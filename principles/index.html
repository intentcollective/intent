Visual Identity Principles of Intent Collective

Why Intent Collective looks and reads the way it does. The meaning behind its marks, and the essential rules of type, color, and space that hold the system together.

The identity and its origin

Design is the manifestation of human intent, and this site is the manifestation of ours. Every mark in the identity carries a deliberate meaning. None is decoration.

Glyphs, typefaces, typography, and visual communication share one lineage. Together they trace the anthropological evolution of human expression, from semiotics through linguistics to visual communication. Semiotics studies how signs carry meaning, linguistics studies the structure of language, and visual communication gives both a designed form. Typography is the most refined and enduring form in which human communication is recorded. That lineage is the foundation of our belief that design is a way of being in every part of human life. Everything is design. We design everything. Design is everything.

This is why our site takes the form and structure of traditional typography. We value tradition not as an old style to imitate, but as the fundamental foundation everything else is built on. Even set in a contemporary sans, the site follows the discipline of the printed page, with a set measure, an even rag, words tied against bad breaks, footnote marks, a Garamond wordmark in small capitals, and the pilcrow.

The logo

Deucalion and Pyrrha. Engraving by Virgil Solis for Ovid’s Metamorphoses, Book I, 347–415. Fol. 7v, image 11. 1563.

In Greek myth, Deucalion, son of Prometheus, and Pyrrha, daughter of Epimetheus and Pandora, were the only two people to survive the great flood sent by Zeus. Told by an oracle to cast the bones of their mother behind them, they understood the mother to be the Earth and threw stones over their shoulders. Deucalion’s stones became men and Pyrrha’s became women, a new human race made by human hands. Ovid tells the story in Book I of the Metamorphoses, and our mark comes from Virgil Solis’s 1563 engraving for that book.

We designers are makers in the same sense. We shape living entities that enrich human civilization and its evolution.

The wordmark

The wordmark is set in Adobe Garamond small capitals. Adobe Garamond was drawn by Robert Slimbach and released in 1989 as Adobe’s first historical revival. It interprets Claude Garamond’s roman and Robert Granjon’s italic, and Slimbach studied the originals at the Plantin-Moretus Museum in Antwerp. Its sources are the same two hands behind EB Garamond, so the wordmark and the manifesto quote speak from one tradition.

Garamond descends from the roman types cut in sixteenth-century Paris by Claude Garamond, who followed the model Francesco Griffo cut for Aldus Manutius in Venice in 1495. These are Garaldes, a name joining Garamond and Aldine. They are old-style humanist serifs with an organic structure close to the pen, restrained stroke contrast, and a diagonal axis. Five centuries of book printing have proved their elegance and legibility.

Small capitals take the form of uppercase letters at the height of the lowercase. Well-drawn small capitals are not shrunken capitals. They keep the stroke weight of the text and are slightly wider, so the whole word sits in one even color. That is the harmony of diversity the collective aspires to, distinct voices in one measure. Their forms are also low and wide. They stand on the baseline and reach about the meanline, the top of the x-height, often rising just above it, so they look stable and grounded. They express the security and stability of the collective.

The typefaces

The site is set in DM Sans, a low-contrast geometric sans commissioned by Google from Colophon Foundry, with creative direction from the DeepMind team, and released in 2019. It was derived from Poppins by Jonny Pinhorn of Indian Type Foundry. DeepMind, now Google DeepMind, is a research laboratory in artificial intelligence. Through it, our text face connects the long tradition of letters with the technology now shaping how we read and write. DM Sans is highly legible, flexible, and adaptable, and its neutral voice reflects the pragmatic and altruistic value of design as a medium of communication.

Yet its lowercase g keeps the traditional double-storey form of the book types. Poppins, its geometric source, uses the open single-storey g. DM Sans redrew it as the double-storey g, and did the same with the a. That one letter shows an understanding of, and respect for, the tradition that holds its value through the ages.

The manifesto quote, “Design is the manifestation of human intent.”, is set in EB Garamond italic for much the same reason. EB Garamond is an open-source revival drawn from the Egenolff–Berner specimen printed in 1592, which shows Claude Garamond’s roman and Robert Granjon’s italic. The italic voice of our founding sentence is Granjon’s sixteenth-century hand, carried into the present.

The double slash

Section titles sit between double slashes, as in // UPCOMING LIVE DEBATES //. The slash descends from the medieval virgule (Latin virgula, “little twig”), which marked a short pause inside a sentence that was still going on. In the Fraktur printing of early modern Europe, the doubled slash served as a dash, joining what came before to what came after.

For us the slash means continuation. One voice pauses so the next can carry the thought further. Two slashes stand for the collective and for collective intelligence.

The color red

Red is the only color in the identity. It is the color of blood and of the human body, of passion, desire, and the sun. It is also among the oldest color words. In Berlin and Kay’s influential study of basic color terms (1969), red is the first hue a language names after black and white.

Red is also the color of the page’s own tradition. Medieval scribes wrote headings and chapter titles in red ink, a practice called rubrication, from the Latin rubrica, red ochre. Our red marks the same things, the chapter titles written between // // and every action. Both are movement. A click is physical, and a new chapter is a cognitive and semantic shift.

The glyphs

Glyphs take the place of letters in the main menu (M△NIFESTO, STRUCTUR三, DISC◉URSE, ZEI↑GEIST) and mark the Foundation (⌗ Strategy, ¶ Literacy, + Synthesis) and the Methodology (∴ Discourse, ⇄ Debate, ⌘ Praxis).

Glyphs are the oldest layer of written communication. Our own alphabet descends, through Phoenician, from pictorial signs. The pilcrow (¶) shows the same evolution in miniature. Medieval scribes drew a C for capitulum, “little head”, to open a new section, then added a vertical stroke and filled the bowl. Reading a glyph as both sign and letter is linguistic typography, the study of how form carries meaning.

The ground

Digital media has no fixed page. Its structure shifts with every screen, unlike a sheet of paper. The faint pattern of squares across the background keeps a modular system in view anyway, like the ruled ground of graph paper. The module is how a designer understands, analyzes, dissects, and organizes a subject. Drawn beneath every page, it shows that our design is grounded in rational visual communication, not in intuitive or spontaneous expression. The pattern is also a measure. Every distance on the site is taken from it.

The system

Intent Collective is set as one cohesive system, a small vocabulary of styles, each with one job, used with discipline everywhere. Emphasis is earned by contrast with a quiet field, never by adding new treatments.

  • Fewer styles, applied consistently. A new style needs a new job, not a new mood.
  • Hierarchy comes from scale, position, and color, in that order.
  • The site, the debate cards, the emails, and the confirmation pages speak the same visual language.
  • Type and emphasis are settled. Layout and spacing are the next step, treated as their own system.

Legibility and readability

Two qualities, two responsibilities.

Legibility belongs to the typeface. It is how clearly each letterform can be told from the others, and it is decided when the face is drawn. Readability belongs to the typographer. It is how easily a whole text can be read, and it is made in the setting, through measure, line spacing, rag, ties, hierarchy, and space.

We chose Adobe Garamond, EB Garamond, and DM Sans for their excellent legibility. The typesetting rules in these principles serve readability.

Typefaces and type scale

One family carries the whole system. DM Sans sets everything, from display to text, labels, and controls. EB Garamond italic appears once, as the voice of the manifesto quote, a deliberate counterpoint to the sans.

The wordmark sits at 28px in the header and 24px at the foot of the manifesto. The logo mark is about 144px.

Line spacing follows the role. Running text sets at 16 on 26, a line height of 1.6. Lists and notes set at 1.4 to 1.5, and headings set tight at about 1.1.

Capitals and small labels get a little added tracking. Lowercase is never letterspaced. Kerning and standard ligatures such as fi and fl are always on.

Figures are proportional lining figures, the set DM Sans offers. They sit at cap height and suit dates and times in running text.

  • Ink #211F1A for text, and soft ink #55524A for secondary text and lead-ins.
  • Brick red #B23A2E is the one color. It marks section titles and every action, the two places where the reader moves.

Three levels of emphasis, each with one job.

  1. Plain ink for normal reading.
  2. Red for a section title, or something you can act on.
  3. A red outline for the one debate that matters next.

No brand logos, icons, or third-party colors enter the field. Company names in Professional Experience and calendar services in the links are set as plain type.

Rag, breaks, and ties

Set flush left, ragged right, with a rag as even and natural as possible.

  • No diagonal or arbitrary rag. A rag that steps in a visible slope, or jumps without rhythm, is poor typography.
  • No widows or orphans. A last line of one or two short words is not acceptable. A last line of three to six words, about a quarter of the measure, is the practical limit.
  • Fix stubborn lines in the copy, not by forcing the layout. Rewriting a phrase beats squeezing the measure.
  • Running text is set to avoid short last lines. Headings and pillar names are balanced, with lines of near-equal length. Balancing is never used on paragraphs or notes, because it shortens lines into a diagonal rag and narrows the block against its neighbors.
  • Ties are the No Break of the web. Non-breaking spaces hold together the names that must not split (Principal Design Fellow, Design Fellow, Intent Collective, US Eastern), keep “a” and “an” with the next word, and tie the last words of each paragraph. Wide paragraphs (480px and over) tie three words and narrow columns tie two, because three would push a whole phrase down and leave a short line above.
  • A heading tied to a short sentence never strands a single word.
  • No first-line indent. Paragraphs are separated by space, never indented.
  • Hyphenate sparingly, and judge by readability. Sometimes a hyphen is unavoidable. The balance between rag and hyphenation is judged by readability, not by rule alone, because the shape of the rag is not only an aesthetic decision. An arbitrary shape pulls the reader’s eye to the edge of the column and lowers readability, and so does a run of hyphens. When a break is needed, a word of fewer than six letters is never hyphenated, at least three letters stay on each side of the break, and no more than two lines in a row end in a hyphen.
  • Flush left keeps word spacing even. Every space between words is the same width, so the typographic color stays even and no rivers open. It also gives the eye one fixed place to start every line, since we read from the top left to the bottom right. Together these make it the most readable setting for running text. Justified text stretches the spaces line by line, so it is not used.
  • Check at several widths (1280, 390, and the narrow in-betweens) and in DM Sans, never a fallback face.

Measure and alignment

  • One measure per role. Running text sets at 64 characters for 16px. Because a character width scales with type size, 13px notes need their own measure (79 characters) to reach the same column width. Without it, footnotes set visibly narrower than the body.
  • Paragraphs of the same role share one measure. A note never sits narrower than its neighbors.
  • Gutters and measure are set once, as tokens, never adjusted element by element.
  • Section rules are 2px and align to the text column, not to the page edge.

Grid, space, and hierarchy

  • Flush left, ragged right, on one axis. Headings, section titles, rules, paragraphs, and cards all hang from the same left edge. Nothing on the page is centered.
  • A column for reading, a field for browsing. Reading happens in one column at the fixed measure. Browsing happens in a fluid field of cards (debates, posts, links), each at least 300px wide, filling as many columns as the screen allows.
  • Columns hang from one line. When equal parts sit side by side, each gets a column of equal width, as many columns as there are parts. The two Fellowship tiers and the two parts of the live debate stand in two columns. The three disciplines of the Foundation and the three methods of the Methodology stand in three, and the four reasons to participate in four. Every column hangs from the same hangline, so the first lines of text start together. We read from the top left to the bottom right. A shared hangline gives the eye one place to start each column, as a flush left edge gives it one place to start each line. On a narrow screen the columns stack in reading order.
  • Space separates, rules divide. Paragraphs are separated by space. Sections are separated by generous space and one 2px rule, and cards by one thin border. Negative space does most of the work, and lines are kept for real divisions.
  • Every distance is measured. Space moves in steps of 8px, and four steps make the 32px module of the ground. Every margin, padding, and gap on the site is one of these steps (4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, or 96px). On a wide screen the page is 35 modules wide and both its edges fall on the lines of the pattern. On any screen the text starts on a line. A border counts as space too. A card’s 1px border and its padding add up to 24px, and so do the next debate’s 3px red outline and the 6px live outline with theirs. Few readers will measure any of this. They feel it as calm and order before they can name it, and that care is what makes a page designed rather than assembled.
  • Hierarchy by position before size. What matters most comes first. When a debate goes live, its Join button grows stronger by moving up, not by growing larger.

The live debate

The system has one state change, and it uses position and weight, not new styles.

  • On the debate card, the order is title, times, the discourse link, then the small utilities. The calendar line and the Join note are set at the 13px note size so the main link leads.
  • When Join opens, one hour before the start, the Join button moves up to sit directly under the title, and the card’s red outline goes from 3px to 6px. The inner padding shrinks by the same amount, so nothing inside moves.
  • On the home page, “Join the live debate” takes the hero’s place for the window, so the one primary action on screen is the right one for that moment.
  • The button itself never changes. Its strength comes from position and from being the only red button in view.

Voice and copy

Copy is part of the typography.

  • Plain, short, declarative sentences on public surfaces. The abstract vocabulary of the manifesto and Structure stays, lower on the page, where it shows the depth of the brand.
  • No em dashes, and no colons or semicolons in the middle of a sentence. Commas and full stops carry the rhythm.
  • INTENT COLLECTIVE in caps uses its own brand style, never a larger size.
  • Verbs over descriptions for actions, such as “Subscribe in”, “Join online” and “Get debate notices by email”.
  • Plain type for credentials. Company names run A to Z, ending in “and more”, with the note that they are individual experience, not endorsement.
  • Footnote marks keep their references distinct, an asterisk for the experience note and a dagger for the image credit.
  • Lists use the midpoint (·), never the bullet (•). The midpoint is quieter and more refined. It does its job without raising its voice. The same midpoint separates links that sit side by side.
  • We use the serial comma, also called the Oxford comma. In a list of three or more, a comma comes before the final “and” or “or”, as in “type, color, and space”. Every item stands equal, and no reader has to guess where one ends and the next begins.
  • Typographer’s quotes and apostrophes, curly and facing the text (‘ ’ “ ”). Straight quotes are never used. Primes appear only for measurements, and an ellipsis is one character (…), never three periods.

Three dashes, three lengths, three jobs. They are never interchangeable, and the keyboard hyphen never stands in for the other two.

  • Hyphen (-), the shortest, joins. It binds words into one compound, as in old-style, low-contrast, sixteenth-century type, and well-drawn capitals. A compound before a noun takes the hyphen, and the same words after it usually do not (a sixteenth-century type, a type of the sixteenth century). At the end of a line it is used only when readability calls for it.
  • En dash (–), half an em wide, spans and relates. The em is a unit of the type’s height. It equals the point size, so in 16px type an em is 16px and an en is 8px. The en dash marks a range of numbers, dates, or time (1510–1561, pages 10–20, 10:00–11:00) and a relationship between two equal terms (a human–AI process, the Seoul–London call, the Egenolff–Berner specimen). It is set closed, with no spaces.
  • Em dash (—), one em wide, interrupts. It breaks a sentence to insert an aside or a turn of thought. Our house style does not use it. Commas, full stops, and a new sentence do that work more calmly.

Accessibility

Readability is for everyone, so the system is held to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2) at level AA.

  • Contrast is measured, not judged by eye. Small text needs at least 4.5 to 1 against its ground. Ink on paper is 12.6 to 1 and soft ink is 6.0 to 1. Red, the color of every action, is 4.5 to 1 on paper and 4.9 to 1 on raised paper. It passes with no margin, so it is never lightened or set on a darker ground. Sage is 4.3 to 1 on paper and 4.7 to 1 on raised paper, so it is used only inside cards.
  • Color is never the only signal. A link is also bold or carries its “>”. The next debate is marked by its outline and its position, and a live debate by its Join button.
  • Every control can be reached and seen by keyboard. Links, buttons, and fields show a 2px red outline when focused. A skip link, hidden until it is reached, leads past the navigation to the content.
  • Targets are at least 24px tall. Buttons, navigation, and jump links meet that size, which is one step of the spacing system. Links inside running text follow the line.
  • Motion yields to the reader. Smooth scrolling and transitions stop when the reader’s system asks for reduced motion.
  • Structure carries meaning. Each page declares its language, headings follow their order, and every image has a text alternative.
